Vulkan Shading Rate in Motion Blur: Visual Trade-Offs

Variable Rate Shading (VRS) in Vulkan allows developers to decouple fragment shader execution frequency from pixel resolution to optimize rendering performance. When combined with motion blur, VRS targets fast-moving regions to aggressively lower shading rates without theoretically harming perceived quality, because rapid movement naturally softens high-frequency details. However, this optimization introduces distinct visual trade-offs, ranging from edge aliasing and banding to temporal instability, which developers must carefully manage to avoid breaking visual fidelity.

How Fragment Shading Rate Interacts with Motion

Through extensions like VK_KHR_fragment_shading_rate, Vulkan permits fragment shader invocations across multi-pixel blocks (such as 2x1, 1x2, 2x2, or 4x4) instead of per individual pixel. The rasterization resolution remains native, meaning depth and stencil testing still happen per sample, but color and material calculations occur at a coarser granularity.

When an object moves rapidly across the screen, post-process motion blur filters high-frequency spatial frequencies into smooth, direction-aligned streaks. Because the human eye struggles to resolve fine surface details on fast-moving targets, reducing the shading rate across these dynamic fragments saves considerable GPU cycles with minimal initial degradation.

The Primary Visual Trade-Offs

Despite the theoretical masking provided by velocity, reducing shading rates in motion-blurred regions introduces several visual compromises:

  • Stair-Stepping and Silhouette Blockiness: While native rasterization preserves the outer geometric silhouettes of objects, internal geometry edges, alpha-tested surfaces (such as foliage or chain-link fences), and normal-mapped details suffer. When a 2x2 or 4x4 coarse shading rate is applied prior to a directional blur pass, the coarse fragments can cause blocky patterns to streak across the screen, producing jagged rather than smooth blur trails.
  • Loss of Specular Highlights and Micro-Detail: Specular reflections, metallic gleams, and fine normal variations contain extremely high-frequency data. Coarse fragment shading calculates lighting once across multiple pixels, which can clamp or dilute intense specular peaks. As a result, objects lose their perceived material roughness, wetness, or metallic shine, causing dynamic highlights to appear dull or completely disappear mid-motion.
  • Temporal Shimmering and Pulsing: If an object accelerates and decelerates, its shading rate may dynamically switch between native 1x1, 2x2, or 4x4 based on screen-space velocity thresholds. This shift creates temporal flickering or pulsing artifacts, where surfaces visibly pop between crisp, high-resolution rendering and blurred, low-resolution rendering.
  • Color Banding in Velocity Streaks: Motion blur relies on blending neighboring color values along a motion vector. If the base image contains coarse shading blocks, the motion blur algorithm samples identical color values across several adjacent pixels. This leads to stepped color transitions and gradient banding inside the motion trail, especially visible under low-light or high-contrast lighting conditions.
  • Haloing and Disocclusion Artifacts: Fast-moving objects reveal previously occluded background pixels. If shading rate decisions are driven by a screen-space velocity buffer, misalignments at the boundaries between high-velocity foreground objects and static backgrounds can cause coarse shading to leak onto static geometry, creating low-resolution "halos" trailing moving entities.

Mitigating Visual Degradation

To minimize these trade-offs in Vulkan pipelines, the fragment shading rate must be applied conservatively. Setting shading rates based on non-linear velocity thresholds prevents over-aggressive downsampling during moderate motion. Furthermore, using decoupled shading rates—such as 2x1 or 1x2 aligned with the primary axis of movement—can preserve sharpness along the perpendicular axis while saving shading cost in the direction of the blur.
