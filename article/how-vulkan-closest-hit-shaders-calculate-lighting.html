How Vulkan Closest-Hit Shaders Calculate Lighting

In Vulkan ray tracing, the closest-hit shader (CHS) is the execution stage responsible for determining the final visual appearance of a surface where a ray intersects geometry. This article explains how closest-hit shaders retrieve vertex data, interpolate surface attributes via barycentric coordinates, sample materials, trace secondary rays for shadows and reflections, and package illumination results into the ray payload for the rendering pipeline.

Triggering the Closest-Hit Shader

During acceleration structure traversal, the hardware tests ray intersections against bounding volume hierarchies (BVH) and primitive triangles. When a ray hits geometry between the minimum (gl_RayTminEXT) and maximum (gl_RayTmaxEXT) hit distances, the pipeline tracks the closest valid intersection. Once traversal completes, the pipeline invokes the closest-hit shader associated with that specific geometry's Shader Binding Table (SBT) entry.

Retrieving and Interpolating Surface Attributes

The intersection hardware provides the barycentric coordinates of the hit point via the built-in hit attribute variable:

hitAttributeEXT vec2 hitAttribs;

Because modern Vulkan ray tracing pipelines predominantly use standard vertex buffers accessed via Buffer Device Addresses (BDA) or descriptor arrays rather than fixed input locations, the shader manually fetches and interpolates vertex data:

  1. Calculate Barycentric Weights: The third weight is derived mathematically: \[w = 1.0 - hitAttribs.x - hitAttribs.y\]
  2. Fetch Vertex Indices: Using gl_PrimitiveID and the geometry's index buffer, the shader fetches the three vertex indices corresponding to the intersected triangle.
  3. Interpolate Attributes: Using the indices, the shader reads positions, normals, tangents, and texture coordinates from the vertex buffer and computes the hit point’s exact values: \[\text{Attribute}_{\text{interpolated}} = w \cdot A_0 + hitAttribs.x \cdot A_1 + hitAttribs.y \cdot A_2\]
  4. Transform to World Space: Normals and positions are transformed into world space using the built-in transformation matrices, such as gl_ObjectToWorldEXT.

Sampling Materials and Evaluating BRDFs

Once the world-space normal, tangent frame, and UV coordinates are established, the shader evaluates the surface's material model, commonly a Physically Based Rendering (PBR) metallic-roughness workflow:

  • Texture Descriptors: Textures are indexed using non-uniform resource indexing or bindless arrays based on material IDs attached to the primitive or instance.
  • Surface Properties: The shader samples albedo, normal, roughness, metallic, and ambient occlusion textures.
  • Normal Mapping: The sampled tangent-space normal is perturbed using the interpolated geometric normal and tangent vectors to compute microfacet surface variation.

Calculating Direct Lighting and Shadows

To determine direct illumination from analytical light sources (such as point, spot, or directional lights), the closest-hit shader executes standard lighting equations (e.g., Cook-Torrance microfacet specular and Lambertian diffuse).

To calculate visibility and shadows, the shader casts secondary rays:

  1. Shadow Ray Setup: A ray is constructed starting from the current surface hit point (offset slightly along the geometric normal to avoid self-intersection artifacts known as shadow acne) toward the light position.
  2. Ray Traversal: The shader invokes traceRayEXT().
  3. Optimized Payloads: For simple binary visibility, the shadow ray typically uses flags such as gl_RayFlagsTerminateOnFirstHitEXT and gl_RayFlagsSkipClosestHitShaderEXT, relying on a lightweight miss shader to set a visibility boolean in the secondary payload.

Handling Indirect Lighting and Secondary Bounces

For reflections, refractions, or full global illumination path tracing:

  1. Ray Direction Generation: The shader samples a new direction according to the surface’s Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF)—either through deterministic reflection formulas or importance-sampled distribution functions.
  2. Recursive or Iterative Tracing: The shader initiates another traceRayEXT() call. In high-performance pipelines with limited recursion depth, indirect bounces are typically handled iteratively in the Ray Generation Shader rather than recursively in the CHS. When recursion is enabled, the CHS suspends execution until the child ray returns its payload.

Writing to the Ray Payload

The primary communication channel between the closest-hit shader and the shader that spawned the ray is the incoming ray payload, declared with:

layout(location = 0) rayPayloadInEXT MaterialPayload prd;

The shader combines the direct lighting, shadow terms, emission, and any accumulated indirect radiance according to the rendering equation. It writes this final computed color and optional path metadata (such as hit distance or surface normals for post-processing and denoising) directly into the payload structure, completing the execution of the closest-hit stage.
