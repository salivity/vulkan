Denoising in Real-Time Vulkan Ray Tracing

Denoising is an essential component of modern real-time rendering, serving as the bridge between raw, undersampled ray tracing data and clean, photorealistic visuals. In real-time graphics using the Vulkan API, hardware constraints limit the number of rays cast per pixel, resulting in high levels of Monte Carlo noise. Denoising filters resolve this issue by leveraging spatial, temporal, and geometric data to reconstruct accurate illumination, shadows, and reflections without incurring the massive computational cost of casting dozens of rays per pixel.

The Real-Time Ray Tracing Problem

Full fidelity in path tracing or ray tracing requires hundreds of samples per pixel (spp) to resolve Monte Carlo noise and converge on an accurate image. However, maintaining a real-time frame rate of 60 or 120 frames per second allows only a fraction of a millisecond for lighting calculations.

Under current hardware limitations, developers using Vulkan can typically only afford 1 to 2 rays per pixel for effects like ambient occlusion, diffuse global illumination, soft shadows, and glossy reflections. Casting so few rays leaves the raw output heavily undersampled, appearing as a severe, dynamic grain across the screen. Without a method to resolve this noise, real-time ray tracing is visually unusable.

How Denoising Functions

Denoising algorithms reconstruct a smooth, stable image by combining multiple adjacent data points rather than tracing additional rays. These filters generally rely on three main strategies:

  • Temporal Accumulation: The denoiser blends information from previous frames into the current frame. By projecting the current frame’s pixels back to their positions in the previous frame using 2D motion vectors, the system simulates a much higher sample count over time.
  • Spatial Filtering: When temporal data is insufficient—such as during rapid camera movement or disocclusion—spatial filters blur neighboring pixels that share similar geometric and material properties, smoothing out variance while preserving high-contrast edges.
  • Variance-Guided and Machine Learning Models: Modern denoisers, such as Spatio-Temporal Variance-Guided Filtering (SVGF) or neural-network-based systems, calculate the statistical variance of the incoming samples or use trained AI models to predict what the fully converged image should look like.

Implementing Denoising in Vulkan

Vulkan provides the low-level control required to execute high-performance denoising pipelines, typically implemented via compute shaders or machine learning execution paths.

  1. G-Buffer Generation: During the rasterization or primary ray generation stage (VK_KHR_ray_tracing_pipeline or VK_KHR_ray_query), the engine outputs primary geometric buffers. These contain surface normals, linear depth, albedo, roughness, and screen-space motion vectors.
  2. Noisy Lighting Pass: Vulkan ray tracing shaders trace shadows, reflections, or indirect light, storing the noisy radiance output in intermediate HDR framebuffers.
  3. Cross-Bilateral Filtering via Compute: A sequence of compute dispatches (vkCmdDispatch) reads both the noisy radiance buffer and the G-buffer guides. The G-buffer ensures the spatial filter does not blur across geometric edges, depth discontinuities, or distinct materials.
  4. Temporal Reprojection and History Tracking: Compute shaders reproject past frames stored in persistent image descriptors (VkDescriptorSet), accumulating valid history and rejecting invalid history caused by dynamic object movement or disocclusion.

Performance and Visual Trade-offs

Denoising fundamentally alters the performance economics of ray tracing in Vulkan. A compute-based denoiser typically executes in 1 to 3 milliseconds, whereas tracing the hundreds of additional rays required to achieve an equivalent signal-to-noise ratio would take tens or hundreds of milliseconds.

However, denoising introduces distinct challenges that developers must manage:

  • Ghosting: Incorrect temporal history rejection can leave trails behind moving objects.
  • Boiling/Temporal Instability: In areas with very low sample counts or high variance (such as rough reflections), residual noise may manifest as flickering or "boiling" artifacts.
  • Loss of Fine Detail: Overly aggressive spatial filtering can blur subtle contact shadows or high-frequency lighting details.

In Vulkan applications, denoising is not merely an optional post-processing effect; it is half of the ray tracing pipeline. By handling the variance of low-sample-count tracing through optimized compute passes, denoising makes practical real-time ray tracing achievable on modern hardware.
