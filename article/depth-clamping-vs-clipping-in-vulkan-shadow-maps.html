Depth Clamping vs Clipping in Vulkan Shadow Maps

In Vulkan shadow map generation, choosing between depth clipping and depth clamping fundamentally alters how geometry outside the view frustum is rasterized. While depth clipping discards primitives falling outside the near and far planes, depth clamping retains these fragments and clamps their depth values to the zero-to-one range. This article explores the technical differences between these two approaches, how Vulkan’s normalized depth coordinates affect them, and why depth clamping is widely preferred to eliminate missing shadow artifacts caused by the light's near clipping plane.

Vulkan Depth Space Fundamentals

Vulkan uses a clip-space depth range of \([0.0, 1.0]\), contrasting with OpenGL's default \([-1.0, 1.0]\). In normalized device coordinates (NDC), a depth value of \(0.0\) represents the near plane, while \(1.0\) corresponds to the far plane.

During rasterization, fragment depth is evaluated against this range. How the graphics pipeline handles primitives with \(z\)-values outside \([0.0, 1.0]\) is governed by the depthClampEnable field within VkPipelineRasterizationStateCreateInfo.

Depth Clipping Explained

By default, depth clipping is active when depthClampEnable is set to VK_FALSE.

Under depth clipping:

  • Any fragment with an NDC depth of \(z < 0.0\) (closer than the near plane) or \(z > 1.0\) (farther than the far plane) is clipped and discarded by the fixed-function rasterizer.
  • The discarded fragments never reach the fragment shader or the depth attachment.

Impact on Shadow Mapping

Depth clipping creates significant issues during shadow map rendering, particularly around the light's near plane. If a shadow-casting object (occluder) is positioned between the light source and the light's near plane, or if it intersects the near plane, the portion of the object in front of the near plane is discarded.

As a result, that portion of the object is never written into the shadow map. When the scene is later rendered from the camera's perspective, the shadow suddenly disappears or develops large holes where the occluder crossed the light's near boundary. This visual artifact is commonly referred to as "shadow detachment" or near-plane popping.

Depth Clamping (Zero-to-One) Explained

Depth clamping is enabled by setting depthClampEnable to VK_TRUE. This requires enabling the depthClamp feature on the physical device (VkPhysicalDeviceFeatures::depthClamp).

Under depth clamping:

  • Primitives outside the clip volume are not culled by the near and far planes.
  • Instead, fragments that would otherwise have an NDC depth \(z < 0.0\) are clamped to \(0.0\).
  • Fragments that have an NDC depth \(z > 1.0\) are clamped to \(1.0\).
  • The rasterizer processes these fragments, and the clamped depth values (\(0.0\) or \(1.0\)) are written to the depth buffer.

Impact on Shadow Mapping

For shadow mapping, clamping to \(0.0\) ensures that geometry positioned in front of the light's near plane still writes into the shadow map at the minimum depth value (\(0.0\)).

When the main scene pass samples the shadow map:

  1. The receiver fragment depth (from the light’s perspective) will almost always be greater than \(0.0\).
  2. The occluder fragment clamped at \(0.0\) will pass the shadow comparison test (\(z_{receiver} > z_{shadowmap}\)).
  3. The occluder successfully casts its shadow onto visible surfaces, completely preventing near-plane clipping artifacts.

Key Differences and Considerations

Feature Depth Clipping (VK_FALSE) Depth Clamping (VK_TRUE)
Out-of-Bounds Fragments Discarded entirely Retained, depth clamped to \([0.0, 1.0]\)
Near-Plane Behavior Occluders cut off, causing holes in shadows Occluders projected to \(0.0\), preserving shadows
Hardware Requirement Core Vulkan baseline Requires the depthClamp device feature
Common Artifact Missing shadows when objects near the light source Potential "false" shadows from geometry behind the light

Managing Geometry Behind the Light

While depth clamping solves the near-plane problem, it can introduce artifacts if geometry physically located behind a point or spot light source is rendered. Because depth clamping treats \(z < 0.0\) as \(0.0\), geometry behind the light can project onto the shadow map and cast unintended shadows forward.

To prevent this when using depth clamping:

  • Tighten the light's projection frustum using culling before submission.
  • In directional lighting or Cascaded Shadow Maps (CSM), position the near plane conservatively behind all potential occluders, or discard fragments in the vertex/fragment shader if their view-space \(z\) is strictly behind the light position.
